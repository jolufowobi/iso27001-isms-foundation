## 1. 🏥 The Situation

The organization had grown fast, but its security had not kept pace:

- No unified information security policy framework
- Access granted informally across two cloud platforms
- No formal incident response process
- Failed customer security audits, with no policies or evidence to show customers

| Risk Area | Why It Mattered |
|---|---|
| 🔒 Ransomware and service disruption | An outage would stop diagnostics and monitoring that hospitals rely on |
| ⚖️ Regulatory exposure | GDPR, HIPAA (for US expansion) and EU Medical Device Regulation obligations for AI diagnostic tools |
| ☁️ Cloud and supplier governance | Two clouds, hospital API integrations and multiple security vendors with no consistent requirements |
| 📋 Customer trust | No evidence to answer customer security questionnaires |

I also reviewed two public healthcare incidents, a ransomware attack on a pathology supplier and a large prescription data breach, to identify the governance failures behind them and carry those lessons into the ISMS priorities.

## 4. 📜 Policy Suite

Every document follows one **standardized policy template**: document control, owner, approver, contacts, classification and review dates.

| Level | Document |
|:---:|---|
| **1** | Master Information Security Policy |
| 2 | Acceptable Use Policy |
| 2 | Human Resources Security Policy |
| 2 | Business Continuity and Disaster Recovery Policy |
| 2 | Data Protection and Privacy Policy |
| 2 | Access Control Policy |
| 2 | Incident Response Policy |
| 2 | Asset Management Policy |
| 2 | Vulnerability Management Policy |
| 2 | Physical Security Policy |

**✅ Control statements:** each of the 167 statements has an **owner**, an **evidence requirement** and an **ISO/IEC 27001:2022 Annex A reference**. They're written to be measurable, for example patching timelines by severity and quarterly access reviews.

**🤝 Third-party management:** suppliers are security-assessed before onboarding, critical suppliers are reviewed every year, and contracts cover security, breach notification, data processing, subcontractors, assurance evidence and exit arrangements (Annex A 5.19–5.23).

**🔄 Business resilience:** business impact analysis, recovery tiers with **RTO, RPO and MTPD** targets, and twice-yearly incident response exercises.

## 7. 📊 Board Reporting

Prepared a **board report and presentation** setting out what was built, why it mattered and what the Board needed to decide.

**Five decisions requested from the Board:**

1. ✔️ Endorse the ISMS scope, policies and SOPs as the organization's security framework
2. ✔️ Approve a risk appetite statement, with low appetite for risks to patient safety and patient data
3. ✔️ Fund the implementation phase
4. ✔️ Commit to certification dates
5. ✔️ Receive quarterly ISMS reports

---

## 8. 💡 Practitioner Notes

I gave every control statement an owner and an evidence requirement so each one can be tested later, and ran the quality review before anything reached approval.

The board report focused on decisions, owners and dates, because a framework only starts working once someone is accountable for each part of it.
